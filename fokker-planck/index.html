The Core Intuition

Imagine dropping a blob of ink into gently flowing water. The ink drifts with the current and simultaneously spreads out due to random molecular collisions. The Fokker–Planck equation (FPE) describes exactly how the probability density of such a system evolves over time.

Instead of tracking every ink molecule individually, the FPE gives you a single partial differential equation that governs the shape of the entire distribution. It's the bridge between microscopic randomness and macroscopic probability.

One sentence: The Fokker–Planck equation tells you how the probability of finding a particle at position x changes over time, given deterministic forces (drift) and random fluctuations (diffusion).

The Equation

The Fokker–Planck equation in one dimension:

∂p∂t = − ∂∂x [ μ(x) p ] + ∂²∂x² [ D(x) p ]

Where:

p(x, t) Probability density at position x and time t
μ(x) Drift coefficient — the deterministic velocity
D(x) Diffusion coefficient — the intensity of randomness (½σ²)

The two terms on the right side have distinct physical meanings. Click each to highlight it:

Drift term

− ∂/∂x [ μ(x) p ]

Transports probability in the direction of the force. Moves the peak of the distribution.

Diffusion term

∂²/∂x² [ D(x) p ]

Spreads probability outward. Smooths sharp features. Increases entropy.

Drift: The Deterministic Push

With drift only (no diffusion), the probability distribution slides rigidly without changing shape. This is pure advection — like a conveyor belt carrying the probability density.

Notice the distribution moves but doesn't spread. Without noise, there's no uncertainty growth — if you knew the position exactly at t=0, you'd know it exactly at any future time.

Diffusion: The Random Spread

With diffusion only (no drift), the distribution spreads out symmetrically. This is the heat equation — sharp features smooth out, and the distribution approaches a uniform spread.

The standard deviation grows as √(2Dt). The peak drops because total probability is conserved — as the distribution widens, it must flatten to keep the area under the curve equal to 1.

Drift + Diffusion Together

Now combine both. The distribution simultaneously moves and spreads. This is the general Fokker–Planck solution: a Gaussian whose center drifts at velocity μ while its width grows with √(2Dt).

Connection to SDEs: The Fokker–Planck equation is the "probability view" of the stochastic differential equation dX = μ(X)dt + σ(X)dW. Every SDE has a corresponding FPE, and vice versa. The SDE tracks individual sample paths; the FPE tracks the probability density over all paths at once.

Potential Wells

When drift comes from a potential V(x) via μ(x) = −V'(x), particles are pushed toward energy minima. The competition between the confining force (drift toward the minimum) and diffusion (spreading outward) leads to a stationary distribution — an equilibrium where drift and diffusion exactly balance.

The visualization below shows a harmonic potential V(x) = ½kx². Watch the distribution start from a delta-like spike and relax toward the Boltzmann equilibrium:

For a harmonic potential, the stationary distribution is Gaussian with variance σ² = D/k. Higher stiffness → narrower distribution. Higher diffusion → wider distribution.

Stationary Distributions

A stationary distribution pss(x) satisfies ∂p/∂t = 0. For a system with potential V(x) and constant diffusion D, the solution is the Boltzmann distribution:

pss(x) ∝ exp ( − V(x) / D )

The ratio V(x)/D acts like inverse temperature. High diffusion (high "temperature") flattens the distribution; low diffusion concentrates it at the potential minimum. Adjust the temperature below to see this in action:

Double-Well Potentials

Things get much more interesting with a double-well potential V(x) = (x² − a²)². Now there are two stable equilibria. At low temperature, the distribution concentrates around both minima. At high temperature, diffusion overwhelms the barrier and the distribution merges into a single broad peak.

This is a model of phase transitions, chemical reactions, and bistable switches. The rate of hopping between wells depends exponentially on the barrier height divided by D — this is the Kramers escape rate.

Applications

The Fokker–Planck equation appears across science and engineering:

Statistical Physics

Brownian motion, polymer dynamics, colloidal suspensions, thermal fluctuations in nanoscale systems.

Finance

The Black–Scholes equation is a Fokker–Planck equation for option price distributions under geometric Brownian motion.

Neuroscience

Distribution of membrane potentials in neural populations. Mean-field models of spiking networks.

Machine Learning

Score-based diffusion models (DDPM, score matching) are directly based on the FPE. The forward/reverse SDE framework uses it to generate images from noise.

Chemical Kinetics

Reaction coordinate dynamics, transition state theory, protein folding landscapes.

Plasma Physics

Coulomb collision operators in fusion plasmas. Velocity distribution of charged particles in electromagnetic fields.

Mathematical Details

Connection to the Langevin / SDE

Given the Itô stochastic differential equation:

dXt = μ(Xt) dt + σ(Xt) dWt

the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation for p(x,t) is:

∂p/∂t = −∂x[μ(x)p] + ½ ∂x²[σ(x)² p]

with D(x) = ½σ(x)².

Conservation of Probability

The FPE is a continuity equation: ∂p/∂t + ∂J/∂x = 0, where the probability current is:

J(x,t) = μ(x) p − ∂x[D(x) p]

Total probability ∫ p(x,t) dx = 1 is preserved for all time, provided J vanishes at the boundaries.

Detailed Balance & Equilibrium

In equilibrium, J = 0 everywhere. For a system with potential V(x) and constant D:

μ(x) pss = D ∂x pss  ⇒  pss(x) = Z−1 exp(−V(x)/D)

where Z is the normalization constant (partition function).

Eigenfunction Expansion

For a confining potential, the FPE operator has a discrete spectrum of eigenvalues 0 = λ0 < λ1 < λ2 < … The eigenfunction for λ0 is the stationary distribution. The gap λ1 sets the relaxation timescale: τ ~ 1/λ1.

Numerical Method Used Here

The visualizations use the Crank–Nicolson finite-difference scheme (implicit in diffusion, explicit in drift) on a uniform spatial grid. This is unconditionally stable and conserves total probability to machine precision. Reflecting (zero-flux) boundary conditions are enforced at the domain edges.
