The Fokker–Planck Bridge

In the Fokker–Planck explainer, we saw how a probability density p(x,t) evolves under drift μ and diffusion D:

∂p/∂t = −∂x[μ p] + D ∂x²[p]

The Black–Scholes equation is exactly this, but viewed backwards in time and applied to stock prices. Where Fokker–Planck asks "given an initial distribution, where will it go?", Black–Scholes asks "given a final payoff at expiry, what is it worth today?"

Fokker–Planck (Forward)

∂p/∂t = −∂x[μp] + ½σ² ∂x²[p]

Evolves probability forward in time. Given where you start, where will you be?

Black–Scholes (Backward)

∂V/∂t + rS ∂SV + ½σ²S² ∂S²V = rV

Evolves option value backward from expiry. Given the payoff at T, what is it worth now?

Key insight: The Black–Scholes PDE is the adjoint (time-reversed partner) of the Fokker–Planck equation for stock prices. The same diffusion process underlies both — one propagates probability forward, the other propagates value backward.

Geometric Brownian Motion

Black–Scholes assumes stock prices follow geometric Brownian motion (GBM):

dS = μS dt + σS dW

This is the SDE whose Fokker–Planck equation governs the probability density of the stock price. The key feature: both drift and diffusion are proportional to S, so the stock price can never go negative and percentage returns are normally distributed.

The Log-Normal Distribution

Solving the Fokker–Planck equation for GBM gives us the probability density of the stock price at time T. Since log(S) is normally distributed, S itself follows a log-normal distribution:

log(ST) ~ N(log(S0) + (μ − ½σ²)T,  σ²T)

Notice the right skew: the log-normal distribution has a long right tail (big gains are possible) but is bounded at zero (you can't lose more than 100%). As volatility increases, the distribution spreads and skews further right.

What Are Options?

An option is a contract that gives you the right (but not the obligation) to buy or sell a stock at a fixed strike price K on or before an expiry date T.

Call Option

Right to buy at price K

Payoff = max(ST − K, 0)

Profitable when ST > K (stock goes up)

Put Option

Right to sell at price K

Payoff = max(K − ST, 0)

Profitable when ST < K (stock goes down)

The question Black–Scholes answers: what is a fair price for this contract today?

Deriving Black–Scholes

The derivation follows three key steps. Click each to expand:

1 Construct a risk-free portfolio

Create a portfolio Π = V − ΔS where V is the option value and Δ = ∂V/∂S is the number of shares held. Over a small time interval:

dΠ = dV − Δ dS

By choosing Δ = ∂V/∂S exactly, we cancel out the random dW term. The portfolio becomes instantaneously risk-free.

2 Apply Itô's lemma to the option value

Since V(S,t) is a function of the stochastic variable S, Itô's lemma gives:

dV = ∂V/∂t dt + ∂V/∂S dS + ½σ²S² ∂²V/∂S² dt

The ½σ²S² ∂²V/∂S² term is the "Itô correction" — it arises because (dW)² = dt in stochastic calculus. This is the same diffusion term that appears in Fokker–Planck.

3 Set portfolio return equal to risk-free rate

Since the hedged portfolio is risk-free, no-arbitrage requires it earn the risk-free rate r:

dΠ = r Π dt

Substituting and simplifying yields the Black–Scholes PDE:

∂V/∂t + rS ∂V/∂S + ½σ²S² ∂²V/∂S² = rV

Notice: the drift μ has vanished! The option price doesn't depend on the expected return of the stock — only on its volatility. This is the risk-neutral pricing insight.

Connection to Fokker–Planck: Under the risk-neutral measure, the stock follows dS = rS dt + σS dW. The Fokker–Planck equation for this process propagates the risk-neutral probability density forward. The Black–Scholes PDE propagates the option value backward from the terminal payoff. They are adjoint operators — two sides of the same coin.

The Formula

Solving the PDE with the boundary condition V(S,T) = max(S−K, 0) for a call gives:

C = S0 N(d1) − K e−rT N(d2)

And for a put:

P = K e−rT N(−d2) − S0 N(−d1)

Where:

d1 = [log(S0/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T)

d2 = d1 − σ√T
S0 Current stock price
K Strike price
T Time to expiration (years)
r Risk-free interest rate (annual)
σ Volatility (annualized standard deviation of log returns)
N(x) Cumulative standard normal distribution function

The term S0N(d1) represents the expected stock value you receive (weighted by exercise probability), and Ke−rTN(d2) is the present value of the strike you pay (weighted by exercise probability). N(d2) is the risk-neutral probability the option finishes in-the-money.

The Greeks

The "Greeks" measure how the option price changes with respect to each input. They are the partial derivatives of the Black–Scholes formula — essential for hedging and risk management.

Delta (Δ)

∂V/∂S = N(d1)

Sensitivity to stock price. Also: probability the option expires in-the-money (approximately). Ranges from 0 to 1 for calls.

Gamma (Γ)

∂²V/∂S² = N'(d1)/Sσ√T

Rate of change of Delta. Peaks when S ≈ K (at-the-money). Highest near expiry.

Theta (Θ)

∂V/∂t

Time decay: options lose value as expiry approaches. Almost always negative. Accelerates near expiry.

Vega (ν)

∂V/∂σ = S N'(d1) √T

Sensitivity to volatility. Always positive: more uncertainty = more option value. Peaks at-the-money.

Rho (ρ)

∂V/∂r = KT e−rT N(d2)

Sensitivity to interest rates. Typically small for short-dated options.

Dynamic Hedging

The Black–Scholes derivation assumes you can continuously rebalance a portfolio of stock and cash to replicate the option payoff. This is delta hedging: hold Δ = ∂V/∂S shares at all times.

The simulation below shows delta hedging in action. A market maker sells a call option and hedges by dynamically adjusting their stock position. The green line shows the hedge portfolio value tracking the option value:

Rebalancing more frequently reduces hedging error. In the limit of continuous rebalancing, the hedge is perfect — this is the idealization Black–Scholes assumes. In practice, transaction costs make continuous rebalancing impossible.

We can verify the Black–Scholes formula by simulation. Generate thousands of GBM paths, compute the payoff at expiry for each, and average the discounted payoffs. As the number of paths grows, the Monte Carlo estimate converges to the analytical Black–Scholes price:

Limitations & Reality

The Black–Scholes model makes idealized assumptions. Real markets violate all of them:

Constant Volatility

Real volatility changes over time and depends on the strike price. This creates the famous "volatility smile/skew." The model underprices deep out-of-the-money puts.

Log-Normal Returns

Real returns have fat tails — extreme moves happen far more often than the Gaussian model predicts. Black Monday (1987) was a >20σ event under the model.

Continuous Trading

Markets close overnight. Stocks gap. You can't rebalance continuously. This introduces hedging error and discrete-time risk.

No Transaction Costs

Every rebalance incurs fees and bid-ask spread. Frequent hedging is expensive. Real delta-hedging is always a compromise between accuracy and cost.

No Jumps

GBM has continuous paths. Real stocks jump on earnings, news, and crashes. Jump-diffusion models (Merton) and stochastic volatility models (Heston) address this.

Known Parameters

The model assumes you know σ. In practice, volatility is estimated from historical data or implied from market prices. This circular dependency is a fundamental issue.

Despite these limitations, Black–Scholes remains the foundational framework. Its concepts — risk-neutral pricing, dynamic hedging, the Greeks — are the language of modern derivatives trading. More advanced models (Heston, SABR, local volatility) are extensions, not replacements.

The Fokker–Planck thread: Every extension of Black–Scholes has a corresponding Fokker–Planck equation. Stochastic volatility models add a second diffusion dimension. Jump-diffusion models add integral terms. The FPE framework unifies them all under a single mathematical umbrella.
